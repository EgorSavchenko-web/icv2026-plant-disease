\section{Problem and question}

PlantVillage is captured in a laboratory: one detached leaf, uniform background, even illumination.
A pretrained ResNet-50 saturates on it, so the question worth asking is not whether transfer learning
works but \emph{which transfer strategy survives leaving the laboratory}. Kumar et al.~[1]
show that fine-tuning can distort pretrained features and lose to linear probing out of
distribution. We ask whether that reversal appears in plant disease recognition, and if so, under
which kind of degradation. The controlled variable is which parameters receive gradients;
architecture, data, optimiser, schedule and seed budget are fixed.

\section{Dataset}

PlantVillage~[2], Kaggle mirror, 38 classes over 14 crops. The distribution ships
\texttt{train} and \texttt{val}; we build the test set by moving 5\,\% of each training class into
\texttt{test} with seed 42, giving 41\,275 / 10\,861 / 2\,169 images. Images are resized to
$224{\times}224$ and normalised with ImageNet statistics; training adds flips and rotation up to
$15^{\circ}$. Model selection uses validation macro F1 only.

\textbf{The split is not grouped by leaf.} PlantVillage holds several photographs of one physical
leaf and Mohanty et al.\ kept them on one side of their splits; our file-level split does not. A
64-bit perceptual hash over all 43\,444 images (\texttt{9\_leakage\_check.py}) finds one exact
match and 51 test images, 2.35\,\%, within
Hamming distance 5 of a training image, 44 of them in the same class. Removing all 51 moves clean
accuracy by at most $10^{-4}$ in either direction, for every run of both conditions; one
linear-probe error per seed falls on a flagged image and no fine-tuned error does. The contamination
is real, measured, and does not carry the numbers. It is a lower bound: the hash catches only
near-identical framing and is not invariant to rotation or mirroring.

\section{Method}

Both conditions start from the same \texttt{ResNet50\_Weights.DEFAULT} checkpoint with a 38-way
linear head. The \textbf{baseline} is a true linear probe: the backbone is frozen \emph{and} every
BatchNorm module is kept in evaluation mode during training, so the features are genuinely fixed
(77\,862 of 23\,585\,894 parameters trainable). The \textbf{second condition} fine-tunes all
23\,585\,894. Shared: AdamW, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch 64, mixed precision,
\texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} on validation macro F1, early stopping with patience 5.

Each condition's learning rate was tuned separately on validation macro F1, since they solve
different optimisation problems. The probe swept $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$ for 0.9644 / 0.9762 /
\textbf{0.9793} / 0.9771 / 0.9734, an interior optimum at $10^{-2}$; fine-tuning uses $10^{-4}$, its
sweep giving 3, 2 and 8 errors with fully overlapping intervals, so no ranking is claimed. Three
seeds (0, 1, 42) per condition.

\textbf{Capture degradation.} We model an autonomous field robot with artifacts applied to the test
images in capture space at three severities, seeded per image: \emph{motion blur}, a line kernel of
5/11/19\,px at a random angle; \emph{low light with sensor noise}, darkened by $0.50/0.30/0.18$ with
Poisson shot and Gaussian read noise at that level, then scaled back up like a camera raising gain:
brightness returns, signal-to-noise ratio does not; and
\emph{JPEG} at quality 30/15/8. Because low light confounds darkening, noise and gain, a
\textbf{control} adds Gaussian noise at unchanged brightness with $\sigma$ matched to the
output-referred noise of low light at mid-grey (0.066 / 0.105 / 0.221 against 0.0657 / 0.1048 /
0.2207). Neither model is retrained.

The reversal is not a sampling artefact. Paired McNemar tests on identical inputs give
$p=1.05{\times}10^{-84}$ at low light severity 2 and $p=6.9{\times}10^{-134}$ at severity 3 for the
probe, against $p=4.8{\times}10^{-40}$ for fine-tuning at motion blur severity 2; across seeds the
best fine-tuned run at low light severity 2 (0.610) still falls below the worst probe run (0.755).

\textbf{The control isolates the cause, not the mechanism.} Gaussian noise at unchanged brightness
reproduces the reversal and produces it earlier --- the probe already wins at severity 1
($p=1.4{\times}10^{-20}$), where low light is a tie. Darkening and gain are not needed; noise is
sufficient. How the noise acts is a separate question this control cannot answer. Inference cost is architectural: measured for both conditions on the same
A100-PCIE-40\,GB worker, 7.09 and 7.30\,ms per image at batch 1, 0.355\,ms for both at batch 64,
773.1\,MB peak memory for both.

\section{Interpretation}

Our hypothesis, not tested here, is that fine-tuning on noise-free laboratory images tunes early
filters to high-frequency texture statistics that only that capture regime produces. Blur and JPEG \emph{remove or quantise} high
frequencies, so the adapted mid-level features still carry the signal and fine-tuning wins by up to
11 points; noise \emph{injects} random energy into exactly the band those filters occupy, with no
generic representation left underneath. The frozen ImageNet backbone, trained on web photographs
that already contain noise and compression, would keep a tolerance fine-tuning trades away; the
direct test is band-limited noise of equal energy~[4]. The \emph{effect} matches Kumar et al.~[1]
(2 points gained in distribution, 7 lost out of it), whose proposed mechanism differs; we refine
it: the reversal depends on the artifact, not on distribution shift in general.

\section{Failure analysis}

\textbf{Clean data.} Every error of the fine-tuned model, two or three per seed, confuses two
diseases of the same crop; for the linear probe the same-crop share is 0.79 to 0.94 across seeds.
Frequent pairs are Corn Cercospora/Gray leaf spot $\leftrightarrow$ Northern Leaf Blight, Grape
Esca $\rightarrow$ Black rot and Tomato Late blight $\rightarrow$ Early blight.

\textbf{Under noise the fine-tuned model concentrates its errors.} At Gaussian noise severity 2 it
makes 969--1\,268 errors across seeds, 61--76\,\% of them on one label, Tomato Septoria leaf spot,
over 16--21 predicted classes. The probe makes 462--567 errors over 21--26 classes, its largest
label taking 22--40\,\% (Target Spot in two seeds). Both drift toward speckled tomato diseases, so
the label set supplies part of the pull and fine-tuning amplifies it. Septoria's fine dark
speckling resembles sensor noise: consistent with the hypothesis, not proof of it.

\textbf{Calibration collapses under shift.} On clean data fine-tuning is the better calibrated model
(ECE 0.0007--0.0016 across seeds against 0.0078--0.0107). At low light severity 2 this inverts:
0.26--0.42 against 0.17--0.20. The model stops signalling exactly when it leaves its operating regime.

\textbf{Limitations.} The sensor model is an approximation: noise is applied to gamma-encoded sRGB,
not to a linear signal, and severities were chosen by inspecting degraded test images. Training times come from different cluster workers.
PlantVillage also carries a background shortcut --- Noyan~[3] reaches 49.0\,\% against
2.6\,\% for chance from eight background pixels. Next steps: a leaf-grouped split, corruption
augmentation, and field images over the intersecting classes of PlantDoc.

\section{Reproducibility}

Repository: \url{https://github.com/EgorSavchenko-web/icv2026-plant-disease}.
Python 3.12, torch 2.10.0, torchvision 0.25.0, CUDA 12.9, NVIDIA A100 under a ClearML agent;
\texttt{requirements.txt} pins every dependency. The test split is committed with a manifest that
\texttt{1\_split\_test.py} reproduces exactly, and release v1.1 carries all six checkpoints behind the
tables plus the BatchNorm ablation, so every number can be verified with \texttt{5\_inference.py} and
\texttt{6\_robustness\_eval.py} without retraining. Corruptions are seeded per image; task ids, workers,
hyperparameters and timings are in \texttt{results/run\_provenance.json}.
